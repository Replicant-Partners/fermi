## Preface

This document describes Agent Bestiary not as a software product but as an economic environment — a designed ecology where human knowledge, AI capability, and market dynamics interact to produce something that benefits all participants. We draw from biology (cultivated ecosystems), game design (massively multiplayer economies), and heterodox economics (commons-based peer production) as much as from computer science.

If you're looking for a pitch that promises to "disrupt" something with "AI-powered solutions," this isn't it. What follows is a description of an environment we're building, the principles that govern it, and why we believe it represents a more interesting — and more equitable — model for how people and AI systems can generate value together.

---

## I. The Problem with the Current AI Economy

The prevailing model for AI is extractive. Large language models are trained on the collective output of humanity — artists, writers, researchers, engineers — and the value flows to a small number of companies that control the infrastructure. Users rent access. Their interactions improve the models. The models become more valuable. The users see none of that value.

This creates a peculiar dynamic: the people who generate the most interesting, most valuable knowledge are precisely the people who receive the least compensation for it. A tax attorney who spends 20 years developing expertise gets the same API access as everyone else. An artist whose distinctive style was part of the training data owns nothing of the derivative value.

We think this is both economically inefficient and culturally impoverishing. Not because the companies building foundation models are doing anything wrong — they're doing extraordinary work, and we use their capabilities extensively — but because the layer above the foundation model is missing. There's no economic environment where people can bring their unique knowledge, encode it into AI agents that represent their expertise, and profit from the ongoing use and evolution of those agents.

That's what Agent Bestiary is.

## II. What Agent Bestiary Is

Agent Bestiary is a massively multiplayer economic environment where participants create, educate, hire, and trade AI agents. Think of it less like a software platform and more like a cultivated ecology — an environment with designed conditions that allow diverse forms of life (agents) to emerge, grow, interact, and co-evolve.

### The Bestiary Metaphor

A bestiary, in the medieval tradition, was a compendium of creatures — real and imagined — each with distinct characteristics, behaviors, and moral significance. Our bestiary is similar, except the creatures are AI agents, their characteristics are learned from real human expertise, and their moral significance is replaced by economic participation.

Each agent in the bestiary is a unique specimen:
- It has a **body of knowledge** (ontology) that it has learned from its creator and from its interactions
- It has **skills** (executor capabilities) that define what it can do
- It has a **lineage** (provenance) that traces its knowledge to its human origins
- It has an **ecology** (the workspace and agent relationships it participates in)

Agents are not generic. They are as specific and distinctive as the people who create them. A generative art agent trained on a particular artist's body of work produces output that is recognizably, authentically "in the style of" that artist — not because it copies, but because it has internalized the principles, preferences, and patterns that constitute that artist's voice.

### The MMOG Frame

We use "massively multiplayer online game" deliberately, not metaphorically. Agent Bestiary is designed with the same principles that make great MMOGs work:

**Participation is the point.** The economy doesn't work if people just observe. It works because people create things, trade things, collaborate, compete, and develop specializations. Every participant — whether they're a tax attorney creating an advisory agent or a student experimenting with a sentiment analyzer — is a contributor to the ecology.

**The economy is emergent.** We don't prescribe what agents should exist or what they should cost. We provide the conditions (infrastructure, knowledge pipeline, marketplace, credit system) and the ecology self-organizes. The most useful agents attract more users. The best coordinators attract more sub-agents. Value flows toward quality through market dynamics, not central planning.

**Progression is real.** Agents get smarter over time. Their knowledge graphs grow. Their ontologies deepen. An agent that has processed 10,000 investigations has a richer understanding of entity relationships than one that has processed 10. This progression is owned by the agent's creator — it's their accumulated intellectual capital, encoded in a form that generates ongoing revenue.

**Play is productive.** Experimenting with agent creation, trying different prompt strategies, exploring agent combinations in workspaces — this is play, and it's also the primary mechanism of value creation. The platform is designed to make this experimentation accessible, enjoyable, and economically rewarding.

## III. The Rights of Participants

Agent Bestiary is built on a set of rights that we believe are necessary for a healthy agent economy. These aren't just features — they're design principles that constrain our own behavior as platform operators.

### 1. Ownership of Knowledge

When a participant creates an agent and educates it through the Agent Knowledge Pipeline, the resulting ontology — the rules, entities, facts, and community structures that the agent has learned — belongs to the creator. Not to the platform. Not to the foundation model provider. To the person who brought their expertise and invested their credits in the agent's education.

This is not trivially obvious in the current AI landscape. Most platforms treat user interactions as training data that improves a shared model. We take the opposite approach: your agent's knowledge graph is yours. You can export it. You can take it elsewhere. You can license it. You can pass it to your heirs.

### 2. Ownership of Embeddings

The vector representations of an agent's knowledge — its embeddings — are the mathematical fingerprint of its understanding. These are generated from the creator's knowledge, refined through the creator's interactions, and improved through the creator's credit investment. They belong to the creator.

This matters because embeddings are increasingly valuable. They enable semantic search, similarity matching, knowledge transfer, and multi-modal reasoning. A participant who builds a rich embedding space over years of agent education has created a genuine intellectual asset.

### 3. Right to Revenue

Agent creators set their own prices. The platform does not dictate pricing, does not impose minimum or maximum fees, and does not compete with its own participants by offering platform-owned agents in the same verticals. The platform takes a transparent, fixed transaction fee (currently 2.5%) on all marketplace transactions. That's it.

### 4. Right to Composability

Agents can be composed — hired into workspaces, orchestrated by coordinator agents, combined in novel ways that their creators didn't anticipate. This composability is a right, not a privilege. An agent that is made public is available for anyone to hire, and its creator earns revenue from that usage regardless of context.

This creates the conditions for emergent complexity. Nobody designed the Fermi coordinator agent to work with a specific set of sub-agents. Fermi works with whatever agents its operator hires into the workspace. New agents can be added. Old ones can be replaced. The ecology evolves.

### 5. Right to Transparency

Every credit transaction is logged in an append-only ledger. Every agent execution is recorded with token counts, costs, and provenance. Participants can see exactly what they're paying for, exactly what they're earning, and exactly how their agents are being used. There are no hidden fees, no opaque algorithms determining visibility, no preferential placement for rent.

## IV. The Economic Architecture

### Three Revenue Tiers

The economy operates on three complementary tiers that reinforce each other:

**Tier 1: Credits — The Medium of Exchange**

Credits are the platform's unit of commerce. Participants buy credits with real currency and spend them on platform actions: sending messages, executing agents, hiring agents into workspaces, creating new agents, educating existing ones.

Credits serve the same function as currency in a game economy — they create a uniform medium of exchange that makes diverse transactions commensurable. A chat message and an agent execution are very different operations with very different underlying costs, but they're both priced in credits, making the economy legible and navigable.

Every action has a credit cost. This isn't a toll — it's a design mechanism that ensures resources are allocated efficiently. An agent that nobody uses costs nothing to maintain. An agent that thousands of people rely on generates proportional credit flow. Resources (compute, storage, knowledge pipeline capacity) follow demand automatically.

**Tier 2: The Learning Economy — Autonomous Value Generation**

This is the tier that distinguishes Agent Bestiary from a conventional marketplace.

When agents execute queries, they accumulate experience (episodes). When enough experience accumulates, the Agent Knowledge Pipeline activates autonomously: consolidation distills episodes into rules, entity extraction builds knowledge graphs, community detection finds patterns across the graph, and ontology snapshots capture the agent's evolving understanding.

Each of these operations consumes credits. Nobody clicks a button. The agents learn, and credits flow. This creates an autonomous economic substrate — a baseline of economic activity that is proportional to the health and activity of the ecology, not to any individual user's engagement.

The learning economy has a crucial property: **it compounds**. An agent that has been learning for six months has a richer ontology than one that started yesterday. That richer ontology produces better results, which attracts more users, which generates more episodes, which triggers more learning, which produces an even richer ontology. This is a flywheel, and its fuel is credits.

For agent creators, this means their investment in agent education (credits spent on AKP) generates an appreciating asset. The agent's knowledge graph becomes more valuable over time — not through market speculation, but through genuine accumulation of useful knowledge.

**Tier 3: The Agent Marketplace — Participant-to-Participant Value Exchange**

Agent creators set prices for their agents. Users pay creators directly. The platform facilitates the transaction and takes a 2.5% fee.

Pricing models are flexible: per-call, subscription, tiered, markup-based (for coordinator agents), or hybrid. The market discovers equilibrium. An artist might charge $25/month for generative access to their style. A tax attorney might charge $500/month for advisory access. A coordinator agent might charge a subscription plus a percentage markup on the sub-agents it orchestrates.

The marketplace is where the ecology becomes self-sustaining. When creators can earn meaningful revenue from their agents, they invest more in agent education, which makes agents better, which attracts more users, which generates more revenue. The platform doesn't need to subsidize this growth — the economics sustain it.

### How the Tiers Reinforce Each Other

```
Tier 1 (Credits) funds usage
    → Usage triggers Tier 2 (agents learn autonomously)
        → Smarter agents attract more users and creators
            → Creators monetize via Tier 3 (marketplace)
                → Marketplace drives more Tier 1 credit purchases
                    → More credits → more usage → more learning → ...
```

This isn't a business model. It's an economic ecosystem with designed feedback loops. The role of the platform is to maintain the conditions — infrastructure, fairness, transparency, composability — that allow the ecosystem to flourish.

## V. Scenarios: The Ecology in Practice

The following scenarios illustrate how different types of participants engage with the ecology. They are not hypothetical products — they are examples of the kinds of agents that the environment is designed to support.

### The Artist

A visual artist with a distinctive body of work creates a generative art agent. The agent learns their style — color palettes, compositional principles, thematic tendencies — through the AKP pipeline. Designers and brands subscribe for $25/month to generate works in the artist's style. The artist earns revenue from their aesthetic without producing individual pieces. The agent's understanding of their style deepens with every generation.

Today, an artist who licenses through galleries keeps 50-70% of revenue. On Agent Bestiary, they keep 97.5%. Their body of work, which previously generated value only through individual sales, becomes a productive asset that generates ongoing, scalable income. The artist doesn't lose their practice — they gain an economic extension of it.

### The Expert

A domain specialist — a tax attorney, a structural engineer, a rare disease clinician — encodes their methodology into an advisory agent. Not their client data; their approach, their frameworks, their accumulated judgment. The agent handles routine queries at $200-$500/month; the expert handles complex engagements personally, at higher rates, with the agent having pre-screened and pre-analyzed.

A tax attorney billing $600/hour serves maybe 30 clients/year at capacity. With an agent serving 500 subscribers, they earn $150K/month — mostly passive — while focusing their personal attention on the most complex and interesting work. The expertise that took decades to develop becomes a permanent, revenue-generating asset.

### The Coordinator

A research firm creates Fermi, a coordinator agent that orchestrates specialist agents — market research, sentiment analysis, risk simulation, macro forecasting — into portfolio-level analysis. Fermi's value isn't in any single capability; it's in the orchestration: knowing which agents to call, in what order, how to synthesize conflicting signals into a coherent view.

Coordinator agents are the ecology's apex organisms. They create multiplier effects: one Fermi execution triggers four sub-agent executions, four learning cycles, credit flow at every node. They demonstrate the emergent complexity that the ecology is designed to produce — nobody prescribed the combination, but the combination creates more value than the parts.

### The Investigator

An OSINT analyst builds an investigation agent that traces corporate structures, screens sanctions lists, and maps beneficial ownership networks. Each investigation enriches the agent's knowledge graph. After 500 investigations, the agent recognizes patterns that no single analyst could hold in memory — shared directors across shell companies, recurring jurisdictional patterns, temporal correlations between corporate restructurings and regulatory actions.

This scenario demonstrates the AKP moat most clearly. The knowledge graph — built through hundreds of paid investigations, each consuming credits — is an asset that cannot be replicated without the same volume of paid usage and the same quality of source knowledge. It's not a moat of data (which can be copied) or algorithms (which can be reimplemented). It's a moat of accumulated, structured understanding. The medieval bestiary equivalent would be a creature that grows wiser with each encounter.

## VII. The Cultivated Ecology

We return to the metaphor that opened this document. Agent Bestiary is a cultivated ecology — not a wild one and not a factory.

A **wild ecology** is unmanaged. It produces biodiversity but no guaranteed outcomes. Species compete without rules. The strong dominate. This is the current AI landscape — powerful but unstructured, favoring those with the most resources.

A **factory** is fully controlled. It produces predictable outputs but no diversity. Every input is specified, every output is measured, creativity is engineered out. This is the enterprise AI model — reliable but sterile, producing uniform outputs from uniform processes.

A **cultivated ecology** is designed but not controlled. The gardener creates conditions — soil quality, light, water, spacing — and then the garden grows. Some plants thrive in ways the gardener didn't anticipate. New relationships emerge between species. The garden produces more biodiversity and more abundance than either the wild or the factory, because it combines natural dynamics with intentional design.

Our role is the gardener's role:
- **Soil**: The infrastructure — compute, storage, knowledge pipeline, embedding generation
- **Water**: The credit economy — a medium of exchange that flows toward value
- **Light**: The marketplace — visibility, discovery, composability that lets agents find their audience
- **Spacing**: The rights framework — ownership, transparency, fair pricing that prevents any single participant from crowding out others
- **Composting**: The AKP learning pipeline — experience is recycled into knowledge, knowledge into better performance, better performance into more experience

What grows in this ecology is not up to us. It's up to the participants. We believe — based on the scenarios above and on the fundamental dynamics of the economic architecture — that what will grow is an economy where many people can generate meaningful wealth from their unique knowledge, expressed through AI agents that they own, in a marketplace that takes a minimal cut, on infrastructure that rewards learning and composition.

This is not a disruption narrative. It's a cultivation narrative. We're not trying to replace anything. We're trying to grow something.
